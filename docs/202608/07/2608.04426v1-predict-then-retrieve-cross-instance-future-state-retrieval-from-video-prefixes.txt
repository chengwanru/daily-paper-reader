## Predict, Then Retrieve: Cross-Instance Future-State Retrieval from Video Prefixes 

Quynh Vo Thong Nguyen † Vinh-Hien Do Cong-Duy Nguyen Anh-Tuan Luu 

Centre for AI Research, VinUniversity National University of Singapore Email: thong.nguyen@u.nus.edu 

Abstract 

We introduce Predictive State Retrieval (PSR) , a task in which a model observes a short video prefix and a temporal question about an object’s future state, then retrieves instances from other videos or images that depict that state. Unlike action anticipation, which pre-dicts a label, moment retrieval, which localizes an observed event within a video, or video gen-eration, which synthesizes pixels, PSR com-bines anticipation with cross-instance retrieval 

across multiple temporal horizons. We con-struct a benchmark from four datasets with graded, human-validated ground truth, diffi-culty tiers, and an oracle ceiling. We also pro-pose LFTR , a lightweight retriever with frozen encoders that predicts a question- and horizon-conditioned future latent and matches it in com-plementary semantic and visual spaces. A ceil-ing decomposition reveals a clear bottleneck: the true future state is highly retrievable once specified, whereas every predictor we evaluate, including a large multimodal language model with access to the prefix frames, remains far below the oracle. Thus, forecasting rather than perception is the central learnable challenge .LFTR narrows this gap at substantially lower inference cost, and ablations attribute its gains to cross-space fusion and hard-negative train-ing rather than latent rollout. We release the benchmark, code, and evaluation scripts. 

1 Introduction 

Anticipating how a scene will change is central to how people act in the world: seeing a glass tip over the edge of a table, we expect it to shatter, and can immediately bring to mind other scenes that show the same outcome. Reproducing this ability has driven a large body of video under-standing. Action anticipation forecasts the la-bel of a not-yet-observed action (Grauman et al.,

2022; Damen et al., 2018; Furnari and Farinella, 2021; Girdhar and Grauman, 2021; Vondrick et al., 2016); world models roll a scene forward in a la-tent space for planning and control (Assran et al., 2025; van den Oord et al., 2019); and video gener-ation synthesizes future frames outright, increas-ingly with retrieval-augmented motion (Zhu et al., 2025). In parallel, video–text retrieval and moment localization let us search large corpora with lan-guage (Luo et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025; Bain et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2026; Lei et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2017; Lei et al., 2021), and vision–language models now describe and reason over scenes at scale (Radford et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Yet these threads stop short of the capability the opening example takes for granted: from what has been observed so far , predict how an object will look next and retrieve concrete examples of that future state from other videos and images. Antici-pation predicts a future but collapses it to a fixed label rather than an open set of instances (Grauman et al., 2022; Damen et al., 2018); moment retrieval searches a corpus but targets a described moment that has already occurred , within a single video (Hou et al., 2026; Lei et al., 2020); composed and cross-instance retrieval match across instances, but from a present query rather than a predicted future (Vo et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Ventura et al., 2024); and world models predict the future but keep it latent and internal , never surfacing match-ing evidence (Assran et al., 2025). No existing benchmark asks a model to forecast a state and then retrieve cross-instance evidence of it — and this gap is what we set out to fill. We introduce Predictive State Retrieval (PSR) 

(Fig. 1): from a video prefix and a temporal ques-tion about an object, retrieve instances — from a corpus of other videos and images — that depict the object’s predicted future state. The target is not observed , so the model must pre-dict a future that grows more uncertain with the horizon; and the answer lives in different videos, so it must be matched by meaning , not appear-ance — retrieving frames that merely look like the present will not do. Because ground truth links instances through their shared semantic state , PSR is language-grounded by design : the bridge across instances is linguistic, and we show empirically (Sec. 5.3) that raw appearance cannot recover this ground truth whereas the semantic-state abstraction is highly retrievable. Our method follows the same two steps a per-son would — anticipate, then recall by meaning .We propose LFTR , a frozen -encoder retriever that predicts a question- and horizon-conditioned future latent from the prefix and matches it against the corpus in two complementary spaces: a semantic 

space for what state is expected and a visual space for how it looks, fused at the score level. Build-ing the predictor on frozen encoders keeps it cheap and transparent, and isolates forecasting as the one component that must be learned. That isolation yields our central finding, a ceiling decomposition :the true future state is highly retrievable and nearly horizon-flat, yet no predictor we run — including a sighted large multimodal model — comes close to that ceiling, so forecasting, not perception, is the core learnable bottleneck . LFTR narrows the gap at a fraction of the cost, with its gains com-ing from fusing complementary spaces and from hard negatives rather than from rolling the latent forward. We make three contributions: • We introduce Predictive State Retrieval (PSR) , a benchmark that asks a model to pre-dict an object’s future state from a short video prefix and retrieve cross-instance examples of it from other videos and images, with graded difficulty tiers, an oracle ceiling, and human-validated ground truth. • We propose LFTR , a cheap frozen-encoder retriever that predicts a question- and horizon-conditioned future latent and retrieves it by 

cross-space fusion of a semantic and a visual match — where what helps is complementar-ity, not count .• We establish, through a ceiling decomposition ,that the learnable bottleneck is forecasting, not perception , and turn the method–ceiling gap into a concrete, measurable open problem — one our cheap retriever already narrows fur-ther than a far larger zero-shot MLLM. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Anticipation, world models, and object states 

Work on predicting the future spans three threads that PSR draws on but differs from. Action antici-pation benchmarks (Grauman et al., 2022; Damen et al., 2018) and models (Girdhar and Grauman, 2021; Furnari and Farinella, 2021) forecast the la-bel of an upcoming action a few seconds ahead — closed-vocabulary classification over a fixed hori-zon, not open cross-instance retrieval. Future-representation prediction — anticipating future visual features from unlabeled video (Vondrick et al., 2016), joint-embedding predictive architec-tures (Assran et al., 2025), and contrastive predic-tive coding (van den Oord et al., 2019) — predicts a 

latent future, but for recognition or planning within 

a video (e.g. procedure planning (Niu et al., 2024)), never as a cross-instance retrieval target with a graded ground truth. Object-state recognition and state-change discovery (Isola et al., 2015; Pham et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2024; Souˇ cek et al., 2022) supplies our state vocabulary but treats state as per-image classification or within-video localiza-tion. 2.2 Retrieval and vision–language models 

A large body of work learns joint video–text spaces for retrieval (Luo et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021; Bain et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022, 2025; Zhu et al., 2024), and video (corpus) moment retrieval (Hou et al., 2026; Lei et al., 2021) localizes a described moment within a video; broad image–text models (Radford et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2023) and multi-modal LLMs (Li et al., 2023; Alayrac et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024) add strong zero-shot recognition and captioning. In all of these the query describes an observed moment; PSR’s query is a prefix plus a future question, so the target has not yet occurred and lives in other videos — re-quiring prediction, not just matching. We use the strongest such encoders as both zero-shot baselines and LFTR target spaces (App. F), and find native video–text alignment does not, on its own, yield a better predictable target than a self-supervised image space. Closest in form, composed image retrieval (Vo et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Saito et al., 2023) — and its video counterpart, composed video retrieval (Ventura et al., 2024) — retrieves a target given a source image/video plus a mod-ification text (“current state + transformation →

target”), which is PSR minus the prediction of the transformation. This invites the objection that PSR reduces to (forecast a state caption) + (text →Xretrieval); our multimodal-LLM baseline ( M3 , §5) is exactly that pipeline, and our fair-comparison study (§5.3, Table 3) shows it underperforms amodel that predicts a native visual latent — a tar-get no text-caption forecaster can reach with any text encoder. 

Closest work and novelty. PSR is not the first task to predict a future (action anticipation) or to retrieve across instances (composed/video re-trieval); its novelty is their union , and that the union is not trivial. Concurrent efforts touch parts but none combine the ingredients: STATUS Bench (Ukai et al., 2025) and CAST (Liu et al., 2026) handle object-state transitions and retrieval but from observed inputs, not a future forecast; com-posed video retrieval (Ventura et al., 2024) matches cross-instance but is given the transformation as text rather than predicting it (our M3 baseline is exactly that pipeline, and loses; §5). To our knowledge PSR is thus the first benchmark to unite 

future-state prediction with cross-instance corpus retrieval across horizons, scored against a graded ground truth and an oracle ceiling (Table 1) — and its ceiling decomposition (§5.3) shows the union exposes a genuinely new bottleneck ( forecasting ), not a solved composition. 

3 The PSR Benchmark 

3.1 Task and Construction Task definition. A query is q = ( x, τ, h ) where x

is a video prefix, τ a question type, and h a tempo-ral horizon. Given a frozen corpus C of instances (video segments / images), the goal is to rank C

so that the ground-truth set Pq ⊂ C — instances depicting the queried future state — appears at the top, for all question types with one model — a capability profile no prior benchmark offers (Ta-ble 1). We report Recall@ {1, 5, 10 , 20 , 50 } and MRR. The graded structure of the GT (difficulty tiers, state granularity, consensus) is exploited by 

stratifying the evaluation (App. M, N) rather than by collapsing it into a single graded score; a tier-weighted nDCG variant is included in the released evaluation code for users who prefer one number. 

Question types. Q1 next state ; Q2 end state ; Q3 

state after k seconds ; Q4 intermediate state (be-fore reaching a named state); Q5 multi-object (the joint future states of several objects). Each maps to a horizon h, so a question is an index into the future trajectory of the object. App. C gives the full taxonomy—per-type definitions, worked examples, query counts, and horizon coverage (Table 6). 

Construction (Fig. 3). From four source do-mains we run a vision-language model (Gemini-2.5-Flash) to inventory objects and their timed state trajectories, generate the five question types, and mine ground-truth instances both within and 

across datasets (plus embedding-similar candi-dates). Candidates pass multi-pass verification (independent multi-frame re-verification and ob-ject/state re-checks), and we additionally release a high-precision consensus pool (intersection of min-ing and verification). Prefixes are real early clips so that the target state is strictly in the future. Full details and prompts are in the appendix; all scripts are released. 

Human validation. Because the GT is machine-mined, we validate it with humans. Five annota-tors (in-house graduate researchers; §Ethics) inde-pendently judged a stratified sample spanning all difficulty tiers and question types — both mined GT positives and top-ranked retrieved candidates — marking each as correct / incorrect / uncer-tain depiction of the queried future state (“un-certain” counted as incorrect, the conservative choice). We report, on two distinct sub-populations of the 500 -query sample, GT precision (fraction of the mined-GT positives confirmed correct) and a 

suspected-miss rate (fraction of retrieved non-GT 

items judged correct, i.e. recall gaps). GT preci-sion is 89 .4% (approx. 95% Wilson CI [86 , 92] ), the suspected-miss rate is 4.8% , and agreement is substantial (Fleiss κ = 0 .76 over the 3-way label; note κ is somewhat optimistic under the “correct”-heavy class balance). The pools are nonetheless re-liable with few corrections; the consensus pool (the intersection of mining and verification, as above) is released for the highest-precision setting. Because that 4.8% suspected-miss rate means mining oc-casionally omits a correct instance, every reported Recall@ k is a conservative lower bound (missed positives can only depress recall), so the method– ceiling gap we analyse is if anything under-stated. 

No temporal leakage and quality. Prefixes are cut strictly before the event: across all benchmark queries the target state starts after the prefix ends in 

100% of cases (a construction guarantee; median prefix-to-target gap 1.9 s). Consistently, prefix-only baselines that do not predict (persistence, zero-shot encoders) score far below the oracle (R@5 ≤ 11 

vs. 53 ): the prefix does not reveal the answer, and the task genuinely requires prediction. Because states, questions, and GT are VLM-assisted, the benchmark can inherit generator biases; we miti-gate this with two independent verification passes, a consensus pool, and the human study above, and flag it as a limitation. 

3.2 Dataset Composition and Statistics Difficulty tiers. The corpus mixes instances from all sources, so for each query the GT is split by 

where the correct instance lives : in-video (the query’s own video, easiest), in-dataset (a different video, same domain), and cross-dataset (a differ-ent domain, the honest generalization test); Fig. 5 shows an example query with its three-tier pool. We further provide subject+predicate (type1) vs. predicate-only (type2) targets and the consensus pool, enabling robustness checks to GT definition.

4.2 Training What is a training sample. The query side is the short prefix video (8 frames → 8 tokens). Each 

corpus/candidate item—positive or negative—is a completed future segment represented by its middle frame (a single still image) in the visual space, or by its canonical state text in the semantic space; positives and negatives are thus single frames (or state strings) standing for future segments, not full clips. Hard negatives are mined from the entire 

218 k-segment cross-instance corpus as the near-est neighbours of the positive in the target space, 

excluding the query’s ground-truth set . They are therefore other instances—overwhelmingly differ-ent videos (in our audited samples 47 /48 ), oc-casionally a different-state segment of the same source clip—never the query’s own labelled future. 

Objective. Only the lightweight head ( ∼12M parameters) is trained, with a multi-query InfoNCE objective using random and hard (embedding-similar) negatives plus a grounding term that an-chors μh to the ground-truth embedding; the en-coder remains frozen and its features are precom-puted.

5.3 Discussion Where the gain comes from (honest ablation). 

Three effects are robust across five seeds. (i) Cross-space fusion is the largest: fusing the semantic (BGE) and visual (DINOv2) spaces lifts R@5 from 

∼12 .8 to 16 .1, and what matters is complementar-ity, not count — a redundant same-type space adds nothing, whereas stacking complementary strong spaces (BGE, Gemma, DINOv2, boundary-SSL LingBot) reaches 18 .6±0.6, a significant +2 .5pp over the 2-space fusion (App. I). (ii) Hard neg-atives give a large, monotone gain in the visual space — mining embedding-similar (visually con-fusable but wrong) future states adds +3 .9pp R@5 (p < 10 −3), saturating near 128 (App. H) — but are neutral in the text-state space, where states are already lexically separated: visual, not semantic, confusability is the binding constraint. (iii) Ques-tion conditioning, grounding, the uncertainty head, and K/H each move a single-space model within seed noise ( ±0.7). 

The multi-step rollout is not the source of the gain (honest negative). Under identical train-ing the full H=8 trajectory and an H=1 horizon-conditioned read are statistically indistinguishable (12 .80 ±0.72 vs. 12 .40 ±1.02 , p=0 .54 ; n.s. at ev-ery horizon), and intermediate-horizon supervision does not help. The gain comes from the future-state head, hard-negative visual training, and fusion — not the rollout mechanism. 

The rollout is nonetheless a rich, under-exploited trajectory. It is not inert: one pass emits the whole future trajectory, and an oracle reading the best-per-query step reveals a large 

+4 .3/+4 .8pp of untapped signal in both spaces — turning that into a robust, oracle-free read rule is an open problem the benchmark exposes (App. L). 

Encoders and tiers. LFTR is encoder-agnostic across eight frozen encoders, and a strong visual target can beat the text-state space (App. F). By difficulty tier, in-video retrieval is solved by appear-ance persistence (R@5 75 vs. ≤ 5 for all semantic methods, including the Oracle) — exactly why we score cross-instance ; the cross-dataset tier ( ≈ 8.4)is the honest generalization gap.

6 Conclusion 

We pose Predictive State Retrieval — predict an object’s future state from a prefix and retrieve cross-instance examples across horizons — and show that a cheap frozen-encoder retriever, whose gain comes from cross-space fusion and hard negatives (not the latent rollout, which we find null), beats a zero-shot 32B MLLM at a fraction of the cost. Its ceiling decomposition isolates far-horizon fore-casting as the core learnable bottleneck — one of several measurable open problems (cross-dataset, multi-object) with large headroom that make PSR a useful forward-looking benchmark. PSR’s oracle ceiling and cheap baseline make it a testbed for the two open problems it exposes: an oracle-free read of the under-exploited rollout, and a cross-modal bridge for dynamic states. Limitations 

The image modality is hard even for the Oracle (R@5 8.9) due to a vocabulary mismatch between video-derived queries and image-corpus states; joint multi-object and far-horizon prediction re-main open. Methodologically, our headline gain is driven by cross-space fusion and hard-negative visual training rather than by a fundamentally new predictor: a controlled isolation shows the multi-step latent rollout performs no better than a sin-gle horizon-conditioned read, and intermediate-horizon supervision does not help. Even end-to-end LoRA fine-tuning of the prefix encoder yields only a small, within-noise gain ( +0 .4–0.5 R@5 across DINOv2 and CLIP) at ∼10 3× the training cost (App. S), so the frozen prefix is a mild constraint, not the bottleneck. We therefore present LFTR as a strong, cheap baseline and—given the flat, high Oracle—position closing the far-horizon prediction 

gap as the central future work. We flag five validity threats explicitly. (i) Single-generator authorship. States, questions, and GT candidates are produced by one VLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash). Two facts bound the resulting con-found: our M3 baseline (Qwen3-VL-32B) is a 

different family from the generator, so the M3 com-parison is not confounded by same-family affinity (cross-family phrasing could in principle penalize M3, but its 11 .9 sits far below the ∼30 free-form ceiling, so it is forecasting-, not phrasing-limited; App. U); and a stronger automated judge (Gemini-3-Flash) agrees with the human labels at 93 .4% 

(Cohen’s κ=0 .78 ) and estimates GT precision at 

88 .6% , matching the human 89 .4% (App. B). The residual concern is that a single generator’s phras-ing conventions may still shape the GT; because that cross-model judge shares the generator’s Gem-ini family, a fully independent cross-family re-authoring of a held-out slice remains future work. 

(ii) The appearance floor is partly by construc-tion. Because GT links instances by shared se-mantic state, a visual-appearance oracle is scored against a semantically-defined positive set; “appear-ance cannot solve PSR” should be read as “cannot recover the semantic-state GT,” not as a claim about a hypothetical visually-defined GT. (iii) Ceiling partition. Both semantic oracles are, by our own audit, mildly optimistic, so while a large method– ceiling gap is robust, we do not claim an exact split of that gap into prediction vs. retrieval error. (iv) Human study scope. Our validation is 500 queries by five in-house annotators (complemented by the automated cross-model check of App. B); it estab-lishes GT precision but is not a stratified, per-tier human audit, and there is no measured human per-formance on the retrieval task itself (so the 16 →53 

headroom is an oracle bound, not a demonstrated human-achievable target). (v) Generalization is cross-source transfer within four domains, not a leave-one-domain-out generalization study. 

Ethics Statement 

The benchmark is built on publicly released video datasets ( OOPS , SSV 2, MOST , HOWTOCHANGE )and is redistributed only as query/GT annotations and precomputed feature indices, under the source datasets’ licenses; we release no raw video we do not have the right to. States, questions, and candi-date GT are produced with a vision-language model and may contain errors or social biases; we mitigate this with two independent verification passes, a high-precision consensus pool, and a five-annotator human study (Sec. 3), and we flag residual model bias as a limitation. The five annotators were vol-unteer graduate researchers fluent in English who judged only object-state depictions (no personal or sensitive attributes); they gave informed consent, were not compensated per-item (to avoid speed incentives), and each labelled the same stratified 

500 -query sample, from which we report Fleiss 

κ = 0 .76 (substantial, though optimistic under the “correct”-heavy class balance; §3). The task tar-gets retrieval of everyday object states and is not intended for surveillance or person identification. 

Reproducibility 

We release the dataset (queries, graded GT pools, splits), the 218k-segment corpus index, all baseline and method code, training configurations, and the evaluation scripts; every number in the paper is regenerated from released eval artifacts.

A Benchmark and Protocol Details 

State extraction and canonicalization. For each segment the VLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash) emits a free-form future-state description, which we parse into a predicate-level state ( type2 , e.g. falling )and a subject +predicate state ( type1 , e.g. ball falling ). Free-form descriptions are lexically noisy — falling , tumbling down , and dropping denote the same state — so we canonicalize synonyms by greedy BGE-embedding clustering: states are vis-ited most-frequent first , and each is merged into an existing canonical cluster if its BGE cosine sim-ilarity to the cluster seed is ≥ 0.85 , otherwise it seeds a new canonical state (the threshold was chosen on a pilot sweep over {0.80 , 0.85 , 0.90 }

to balance merge rate against semantic coherence, keeping mean cluster size ≤5). Canonicalization reduces the raw state vocabulary to 51 ,362 canoni-cal predicate-level states, and every corpus segment and query target is re-keyed to its canonical state; all retrieval and ground truth are defined at this canonical level. 

Ground-truth mining. For a query whose tar-get is canonical state s, we mine three comple-mentary GT pools: in-dataset (a different video of the same domain whose segment carries canonical state s), cross-dataset (a segment from a differ-ent domain with canonical state s — the honest cross-instance test), and bge-similar (BGE neigh-bours of s at cosine ≥ 0.85 , a looser recall-oriented pool). Candidates then pass multi-pass VLM re-verification (independent multi-frame object / state re-checks); the released high-precision consensus 

pool is the intersection of the mining and verifica-tion passes. Structured hard negatives for training are the nearest BGE / visual neighbours of the tar-get that are excluded from every GT pool (App. H). Because mining is embedding-based and canonical, a 4.8% suspected-miss rate remains (§3), so every Recall@ k is a conservative lower bound. 

Single-prefix protocol. Each (video, object) contributes one fixed early prefix (8 frames be-fore the event); multiple queries—across question types and horizons—share that prefix, so a method must predict rather than exploit a late, near-answer prefix. (We adopt this over the alternative of cut-ting a fresh near-answer prefix per horizon, which would leak the target; we use the single-prefix pro-tocol throughout.) Ground-truth definitions. States come in two granularities: type2 (predicate only, the default index) and type1 (subject + predicate, stricter). A high-precision consensus pool (the intersection of mining and verification, §3) is also provided. Method rankings are stable across all definitions (App. N). 

B Cross-Model GT Validation 

Beyond the five-annotator human study (§3), we cross-check the same 500 -query sample with a stronger, automated judge — Gemini-3-Flash — prompted with the identical cor-rect / incorrect / uncertain rubric. It agrees with the human majority label at 93 .4% (Cohen’s κ=0 .78 ,on par with the 5-annotator κ=0 .76 ) and indepen-dently estimates GT precision at 88 .6% , corroborat-ing the human 89 .4% (Table 5). This thickens the reliability evidence with an automated second opin-ion. Because the judge shares the generator’s Gem-ini family, it does not substitute for a fully indepen-dent cross-family re-authoring (Limitation (i)); the M3 baseline (Qwen3-VL-32B), however, is cross-family from the generator, so the headline method-vs-MLLM comparison is already unconfounded by same-family affinity. 

C Question-Type Taxonomy 

PSR poses five temporal question types over each (video, object) prefix, each probing a different facet of future-state prediction (Table 6; a worked exam-ple per type in Fig. 7). Q1–Q4 constitute the main benchmark ( 20 ,182 queries; one shared early pre-fix per (video, object), queried at several horizons and types); Q5 is a separate multi-object stress test (App. Q). All types are generated by prompting a VLM over each object’s timed state trajectory 

{(state i, t i)} and are answered against the same graded, cross-instance ground truth. Per-type re-trieval results are in Table 7. 

Q1 — Next state. The immediately following 

state after the prefix. Almost always a single step ahead ( h=1 for 98% of Q1), so it is the shortest-horizon, most perceptually anchored type, yet still requires predicting a transition rather than copying the present. 

Q2 — End state. The object’s final state at the end of the video. Because “the end” is a variable distance away, Q2 spans the full horizon range 

(h=1 –7) and is the hardest type (Table 7): the far future is genuinely multi-modal. 

Q3 — After k seconds. The state a fixed k seconds ahead; k maps to a prediction horizon. Tests fixed-lead-time forecasting and, with k varied, gives the horizon-stratified curves in Fig. 6(b). 

Q4 — Intermediate state. A state the object 

passes through before reaching a named later state (“before losing balance, what state does it pass through?”). The largest type ( 7,981 ), it probes trajectory/ordering reasoning rather than endpoint prediction. 

Q5 — Multi-object. The joint future states of ≥ 2

objects in the scene, scored per object and jointly (App. Q); a compositional stress test kept separate from the single-object main benchmark.

Does fusing prefix encoders help? No—a re-vealing asymmetry. Cross-space fusion helps on the target (retrieval) side (App. I); we test whether it also helps on the prefix (encoding) side by con-catenating the per-frame features of multiple en-coders as the LFTR prefix (DINOv2 target; Ta-ble 10). It does not : the single strongest encoder, Qwen-VL, beats every fused prefix, and mixing in weaker per-frame features (DINOv2, SigLIP, and especially V-JEPA2) monotonically dilutes it. This asymmetry is informative: the target space is a 

retrieval index that benefits from complementary views (semantic + visual cover different queries), whereas the prefix is a single input encoding best served by the one strongest representation—adding weaker, redundant feature dimensions only gives the frozen head more to overfit. Fusion helps where views are complementary, not where one view al-ready dominates. 

Best zero-shot encoder ̸ = best target space (a target-space bake-off). We fix the strongest prefix encoder (Qwen-VL) and vary only the retrieval tar-get space, spanning a text-state space (BGE), image encoders (SigLIP, DINOv2), and two recent video-native video–language foundation models (Lan-guageBind (Zhu et al., 2024), InternVideo2 (Wang et al., 2025)), each encoding the corpus segment as an 8-frame clip (Table 11). Two things stand out. First, the zero-shot retrieval ranking is es-sentially inverted relative to the LFTR-target rank-ing : SigLIP is the best zero-shot retriever ( 7.74 )but the worst learned target of these five (10 .6), whereas DINOv2 is the worst zero-shot retriever (5.47 ) yet the best of the five (12 .8). Second, the two newest video–language models, despite native temporal modeling and strong zero-shot ( 6.8–7.1), do not beat DINOv2 as targets ( 11 .0/11 .4). The reason is consistent: image–text/video–text con-trastive features are optimized to match present appearance and are excellent zero-shot, but they are a harder, less structured target for a predictor to regress toward; self-supervised patch features re-trieve poorly zero-shot yet are the most predictable 

future-state target. The clearest case is LingBot-Vision (“LingBot” for short) (Fu et al., 2026), an SSL image encoder trained with masked bound-ary modeling: it is the weakest zero-shot retriever (4.94 , below DINOv2) yet the best learned target of all ( 13 .6±0.2 over 5 seeds, above DINOv2’s 12 .8)— a spatial-/boundary-aware self-supervised objec-tive yields the most predictable target. Tellingly, the same LingBot encoder makes only a mid-tier prefix (9.75 as a prefix encoder, App. F, below Qwen-VL/InternVideo2/SigLIP/DINOv2): being a good target (a predictable retrieval index) and a good prefix (a rich input encoding) are differ-ent properties. Choosing a target space is thus a modeling decision about predictability , not a race for the best off-the-shelf retriever — and no purely visual space, video-native or not, closes the gap to the semantic ceiling, reinforcing our language-grounded thesis (Sec. 5.3). The seman-tic axis quality is a lever. L Rollout: A Rich but Under-Exploited Trajectory 

The rollout is not an accuracy lever (App. K), but it is a structured predictor with a distinc-tive, if untapped, property. (i) Single-pass, any-horizon. One rollout emits the full future trajec-tory (μ1, . . . , μ H ), so a shared single early prefix— queried at many horizons and question types—is answered by reading different steps of one forward pass, rather than recomputing per query. (ii) The trajectory is rich. Table 17 varies only which step of a fixed trained model is read. A horizon-agnostic 

fixed step 1 read underperforms the default matched step h read, confirming the trajectory is horizon-ordered. Crucially, an oracle that reads the best step per query reaches R@5 17 .7 (DINOv2) / 16 .0

(BGE) vs. 13 .4 / 11 .2 for the matched read—a large 

+4 .3/+4 .8pp head-room that replicates across both retrieval spaces . (iii) The signal is per-query and hard to harvest. A validation-learned per-horizon step calibration adds ≈ 0pp (the best step is query-, not horizon-, specific), and a multi-hypothesis read (max-similarity over all steps at once) helps only at far horizons in the visual space ( h≥3: 7.2

vs. matched 5.1, +2 .1pp; near horizons and BGE: neutral-to-worse). So the rollout demonstrably en-codes retrievable future states across its steps, but converting that latent richness into a robust, oracle-free read rule is an open problem the benchmark surfaces—a concrete future direction rather than a solved contribution.

R Efficiency 

The LFTR head is 11 .98 M parameters and infer-ence is 0.06 ms/query (predict + retrieve) with the encoder frozen and features precomputed — roughly four orders of magnitude cheaper than gen-erating with a 32 B MLLM per query. Table 19 gives the full budget on a single NVIDIA H200: because encoders are frozen and their features pre-computed once, training the head is cheap ( < 1

GPU-hour per run) and the whole multi-seed, multi-space study is only a few GPU-hours; the dominant one-time cost is precomputing the corpus feature indices, which we release so the benchmark repro-duces without re-encoding. 

S Does Unfreezing the Encoder Help? 

We probe whether LFTR is limited by its frozen prefix, its objective, or its target. Objective/target changes are null. Redirecting the grounding loss from the query’s GT-instance centroid to the state prototype the Oracle uses (a natural “align with the ceiling” idea) changes R@5 by −0.02 over five seeds ( p=0 .96 ); the multi-step rollout is sta-tistically indistinguishable from a single horizon-conditioned read; and intermediate-horizon super-vision does not help at any weight. Only un-freezing the encoder helps, and only slightly. 

We LoRA-fine-tune the prefix encoder end-to-end through the LFTR head, with a frozen-encoder run of the same script as the control (Table 20). Un-freezing gives +0 .53 (DINOv2) and +0 .37 (CLIP) R@5 — consistent across two encoders but within single-seed noise ( ±0.7) and at ∼40 min/epoch ver-sus seconds/epoch for a frozen head on cached features. The frozen prefix is therefore a mild con-straint; even a fully adaptable encoder stays ∼45 pp below the horizon-flat Oracle, so far-horizon pre-diction — not the encoder, head, or loss — is the binding bottleneck. Frozen-LFTR is the efficient Pareto point.

U Retrieval-Channel Decomposition 

The raw method–ceiling gap ( 16 → 53 ) mixes two distinct sub-problems, and separating them clari-fies what a better predictor can actually close. All learned methods retrieve a free-form predicted state string, whereas the semantic oracle ( 53 ) retrieves the dataset’s canonical string; so part of the gap is a retrieval-canonicalization channel (how well a correct-but-differently-phrased state matches the canonical index), not forecasting. 

Forecasting vs. canonicalization (clean con-trol). We paraphrase each query’s true state with an LLM (reword, same meaning) and retrieve the paraphrase. This holds the content correct and varies only the phrasing , so any drop from 53 is pure canonicalization loss. The paraphrase oracle reaches R@5 29 .7 (Table 21). Two consequences: (i) the ceiling achievable by any free-form fore-caster is ∼30 , not 53 ; (ii) the genuine forecasting 

gap a better predictor can close is therefore ∼14 pp (16 → 30 ), while the 30 → 53 residual is an arti-fact of exact-string canonicalization — a lever for future benchmark design (e.g., paraphrase-robust GT indices), not for the predictor. This sharpens rather than weakens the “prediction is the open problem” message: it gives a concrete, achievable target ( 30 ) and isolates the learnable portion of the gap.
